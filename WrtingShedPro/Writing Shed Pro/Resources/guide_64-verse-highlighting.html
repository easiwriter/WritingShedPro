Understanding Meter

Meter is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of poetry:

  • Unstressed (˘): Lighter, shorter sounds
  • Stressed (´): Heavier, emphasized sounds

Working with Stress Patterns

Verifying Meter

For formal verse: 1. Write your line 2. Check the stress display 3. Read aloud to confirm 4. Adjust problem areas

Common Issues

Wrong stress on a word - Try a synonym - Rearrange the phrase - Consider if the "error" works poetically

Extra or missing syllables - Adjust word choices - Use elision when appropriate - Consider metrical substitution

Metrical Substitution

Poets regularly substitute feet for effect: - A trochee in iambic verse for emphasis - A spondee for weight - A pyrrhic (˘ ˘) for lightness

Good poetry varies the beat—perfect regularity can sound mechanical.

Stress in Different Forms

Sonnets

Traditionally iambic pentameter: - 10 syllables per line - Alternating unstressed-stressed - Substitutions common and encouraged

Blank Verse

Iambic pentameter without rhyme: - Shakespeare's plays - Milton's Paradise Lost - Flexibility within the pattern

Ballad Meter

Alternating tetrameter and trimeter: - Common in folk songs - "Common meter" in hymns

Free Verse

No required meter: - Use stress for emphasis - Create rhythmic phrases - Contrast regular and irregular lines

Tips for Metered Verse

Read Shakespeare

The master of iambic pentameter. Note where he follows and breaks the pattern.

Read Aloud

Always. The stress display is a guide—your voice is the test.

Embrace Variation

Perfect meter sounds like a metronome. Variation creates life.

Trust Your Ear

If it sounds right, it probably is—regardless of what the analysis shows.

Learn Natural Stress

English has natural rhythms. Learn which words are typically stressed.

Disabling Stress Display

If the stress marks distract you: 1. Toggle off in poem settings 2. Re-enable when checking meter 3. Use as a tool, not a constant presence
